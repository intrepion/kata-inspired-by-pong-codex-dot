# Original TypeScript paddle collision timing

The original browser game has its own TypeScript simulation, separate from the shared Rust core. Its old collision logic tested the ball and paddles at the end of a step rather than where and when the ball crossed a paddle face. A failing-before example starts the ball at `(60, 180)` with velocity `(-350, 600)` for 0.033 seconds against a centered player: at the face the ball is about `y=193.7`, outside the paddle's `197..303` collision range, but its end-of-step height is `199.8` and the old code incorrectly bounces it. A separate moving-paddle case also falsely bounced when the paddle arrived after contact.

The independent TypeScript model now evaluates ball height and paddle position at face-crossing time, accounts for a preceding wall reflection, and advances the reflected ball for the remaining step with a possible wall reflection. The shared Rust physics and its reference were not changed. Four focused regressions cover hit/miss and moving paddles on both sides, residual travel, top/bottom wall reflection before and after impact, tangent contact and zero horizontal movement. All 23 Node model and mocked-handler tests plus the pinned TypeScript build passed on Omarchy ARM64; generated `game.js` was rebuilt. The model assumes at most one wall crossing per bounded step at normal game speeds.

Separate deterministic original-TypeScript [before](evidence/original-ts-collision-before-20261113.json) and [after](evidence/original-ts-collision-after-20261113.json) model outputs are retained; neither replaces historical browser or Rust evidence. Actions and all 13 reported checkpoints' state and scores match. First ball divergence is tick 571 at the CPU face: old x `848`, new x approximately `846.567` after remaining travel; the impact angle also changes from sampling at contact. The new trajectory scores one tick earlier at tick 895, so intermediate scores briefly differ even though the next 120-tick reports match. Ball fields differ at reported ticks 600, 720, 840, 1080, 1200 and 1440. This comparison is model-driven and not an app capture, a physical collision interaction, or cross-language numerical parity.
